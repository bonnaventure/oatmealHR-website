You send your applicant pool to a screening service, and a day later you get back a document. What should that document actually contain?

That's the question every hiring manager should ask before trusting any assessment tool with their pipeline. A candidate shortlist report is the deliverable that turns raw applicant responses into a decision-ready ranking—but not all reports are created equal, and some are actively dangerous if taken at face value.

## What a shortlist report is

A candidate shortlist report summarizes a group of applicants who completed the same structured assessment, ranks them by fit for a specific role, and shows the evidence behind each placement. Its job is simple: tell you who to interview first, why, and what to probe during the interview.

## What a good report includes

**A ranked shortlist.** Candidates ordered by role fit, typically grouped into tiers—who to interview, who to consider, and who likely isn't right for this role. One page, scannable, no jargon.

**Individual profiles.** For each candidate, scores across defined traits (for us: competence, hard work, trustworthiness, and reliability), with direct quotes and evidence from their own answers supporting each score.

**Strengths and risks.** Not just totals. A candidate might reason exceptionally well but show weak follow-through—a combination that matters enormously depending on the role.

**Red flags.** Honest ambiguity, blame-shifting, or answers that don't match the resume deserve to be surfaced, buried, or questioned—not silently averaged away.

**Recommended interview questions.** The best reports don't end at ranking; they hand you tailored questions to validate each candidate's specific signals in the interview itself.

**Confidence notes.** Assessments sample behavior, not destiny. A trustworthy report distinguishes strong evidence from thin evidence instead of dressing every score in false certainty.

## Red flags in the reports you receive

Be wary of any tool that:

- Presents scores as verdicts rather than inputs—"92% match" implies a precision that behavioral sampling cannot honestly claim.
- Automatically rejects candidates. Decision support is fine; silent elimination is legally risky and dehumanizing.
- Can't explain *why* someone scored what they did. If there's no evidence trail, it's a black box pointed at your hiring process.
- Claims to remove bias entirely. Responsible tools reduce subjectivity and disclose their limits—they don't promise perfection.

## How to use one well

Treat the report as the start of the interview, not the end of it. Read the ranking, skim the evidence for your top candidates, and bring the recommended questions into the room. Then compare what the assessment predicted against what you observe live—that feedback loop is what makes each round of hiring sharper than the last.

Used this way, a shortlist report does something resumes never could: it lets you spend your limited interview time on the handful of candidates whose thinking patterns suggest they'll actually show up, take ownership, and follow through.
