Every hiring team has lived the same scenario: a candidate interviews brilliantly, gets the job, and three weeks later you're wondering how you missed the warning signs. The interview went well because interviewing well is a learnable skill—not because the candidate shows up on time, owns their mistakes, or follows through when the work gets repetitive.

## Resumes tell you what candidates claim, not how they operate

A resume is a marketing document. References are curated lists of people who will say nice things. And standard behavioral questions—"Tell me about a challenge you overcame"—have been answered so many times in YouTube videos and interview-prep blogs that most candidates simply recite polished scripts.

The signals that actually predict performance in practical roles are different:

- **Reliability.** Do they show up, on time, prepared, day after day?
- **Trustworthiness.** Do they take accountability or deflect blame?
- **Hard work.** Do they persist when tasks are difficult or boring?
- **Competence.** Can they reason clearly and explain what they did and why?

None of these announce themselves on a resume, and all of them can be faked for 30 minutes in an interview.

## Rehearsed answers hide real behavior

The problem isn't that candidates prepare—it's that preparation rewards performance over substance. When everyone knows the "right" story about leadership and conflict resolution, those answers stop carrying information. You end up comparing polish, not potential.

This is especially costly for hourly, shift-based, and operations roles, where the difference between a good hire and a bad one is measured in missed shifts, rework, and rehiring costs—not in how eloquently someone describes their greatest weakness.

## Unusual questions reveal real thinking

This is why we ask candidates practical, open-ended questions they cannot rehearse: *How do you make a bowl of oatmeal? What kind of alarm clock do you use? You need to move a pile of sand with a teaspoon—what do you do in hour one versus hour ten?*

There are no trick questions and no perfect answers. A simple process question reveals planning and attention to detail. A preparation question reveals conscientiousness. An ethical metaphor reveals accountability. What you're hearing is structure, clarity, honesty, and practical judgment—the real behavior underneath the résumé.

## Keep interviews. Change what happens before them.

Interviews still matter—they're where you validate signals, not discover them from scratch. A ranked shortlist built from structured behavioral evidence means you interview fewer candidates, ask better questions targeted at each candidate's risks and strengths, and are far less likely to be surprised after the offer goes out.

Traditional hiring isn't broken, but it is shallow. If you want to know who will actually show up, screen for how people think before you judge how they talk.
